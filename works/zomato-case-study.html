01 — Overview

A shared cart.
No middleman.

Group food orders almost always start the same way: someone creates a WhatsApp group, everyone types out what they want, and one unlucky person manually adds 4–5 people’s orders into a single cart. It works, barely — and it quietly costs the app money every time it happens.

This project explores what it would look like if Swiggy or Zomato let a group build one shared cart inside the app itself, with the bill splitting automatically at checkout. No coordination outside the app. No manual math after the food arrives.

02 — The Problem

Coordination
outside the app.

When a group orders from a single restaurant, there is no way to combine everyone’s items inside the app. Coordination happens entirely outside it — and it is messier than it sounds.

01
The WhatsApp collect
Someone creates a group or sends a voice note asking what everyone wants. People reply at different times, often with edits (“actually skip the naan”). There is no audit trail, no structure.
02
The manual cart build
One person retypes every order into a single cart. This person becomes the unofficial “order admin” — a role nobody volunteered for and that nobody wants twice in a row.
03
The post-delivery math
After the food arrives, someone calculates who owes what. Delivery fees and add-ons get glossed over. Someone always ends up overpaying or underpaying. A UPI request gets sent eventually.
04
Why this matters for the business
Group orders likely carry a higher average order value than solo orders. If the in-app group ordering experience were smoother, groups that currently order individually might start ordering together — and groups already ordering together might do it more often.
03 — Research

Five conversations.
One clear pattern.

I ran 5 informal interviews (15–20 minutes each) with people who order food in groups at least twice a month — roommates, coworkers ordering lunch, friend groups. I used a Google Form to screen participants, then took notes during calls rather than recording them. Lower friction; people talked more openly.

Finding Detail
Coordination happens outside the appAll 5 participants use WhatsApp or voice notes to collect orders before opening the delivery app
One person becomes the order admin4 of 5 said the same person usually ends up typing everyone’s order manually into the cart
Bill splitting is done after the factAll 5 calculate who owes what only after the food arrives, via mental math or a UPI request sent later
Trust in automatic splitting is mixed3 of 5 would trust an even split, but were sceptical of accurate item-level splitting
Forgotten items are common3 of 5 recalled at least one instance where someone’s item was left out of the cart
“I always end up typing everyone’s order into one cart myself. It’s so annoying, because by the time I’m done, someone’s changed their mind about what they wanted.” — Participant, 22. Shares an apartment with 3 roommates.
04 — Persona

Who we’re
designing for.

Patterns from the five interviews converged on a single archetype: someone who orders in groups regularly and keeps ending up as the person who organises everything.

Priya, 23
Marketing Executive — Orders with 3 roommates ~2× a week

Priya orders food with her roommates on weeknights when nobody wants to cook. She’s often the one who ends up coordinating because she’s fastest on the app. She doesn’t mind splitting bills manually, but finds it tedious enough that she sometimes just orders her own food separately to avoid the hassle — which means the group orders less often than they otherwise would.

Goals

  • Order with her roommates without becoming the unofficial “order admin” every time
  • Know the bill is split fairly without doing the math herself
  • Place the order quickly before everyone gets too hungry to agree

Frustrations

  • Retyping orders she didn’t place herself
  • Chasing people for money after the food arrives
  • Items getting missed because someone replied late
05 — Defining the Solution

Before Figma.
A mini PRD.

Before opening Figma I wrote this out like a product requirements document. The structure of a feature matters before the pixels do.

Problem statement
Groups ordering food together have no in-app way to combine orders or split costs, forcing manual coordination outside the app and discouraging some groups from ordering together at all.

Success metric
Average order value on orders tagged as “group orders,” measured against solo orders from the same users. Secondary: frequency of group orders per user per month, pre- and post-launch.

v1
In scope
Shareable link that opens a shared cart · Live updates as members add items · Automatic bill split by item + even split for shared fees · “Lock order” step to prevent indefinite changes
v2
Out of scope (later)
In-app group chat · Saved recurring groups · Group order history · Multi-restaurant group orders
06 — Wireframes

Structure first,
pixels second.

Before any colour or final UI, I sketched the three core screens in low fidelity to nail the structure. Deliberately gray boxes and placeholder text — the goal was only to confirm the layout made sense before committing to design.

I walked 2 of the original interview participants through the wireframes verbally and asked them to react to each screen. Their main feedback: the bill split screen needed to show an item-level breakdown, otherwise it wouldn’t feel trustworthy. I built that into the first high-fidelity version.

W1
Start group order
Entry point from the restaurant page — a single button that creates a shareable cart alongside the regular “add to cart” button. The action is additive, not a replacement.
W2
Live group cart
Shows who’s joined, what they’ve added, and a way to add your own items. Each person’s additions are colour-coded so the cart doesn’t feel like a single undifferentiated list.
W3
Bill split
Shown after the order is locked. Breaking down what each person owes. This was the screen that required the most iteration between wireframe and final design.
07 — Design Iteration

Testing taught
more than assuming.

Version 1 of the bill split screen listed every single item next to the person who added it, followed by a subtotal per person. This matched the wireframe feedback about wanting to see item-level detail.

“I trust it more seeing the items, but honestly I just want to know the number I owe — I don’t need to scroll through everyone’s order to find mine.”

Version 2 keeps the item breakdown, but moves it behind a “tap to see items” interaction. The default view shows only the final amount owed per person. The trust-building detail is still available — it just doesn’t dominate the screen.

This is a small change, but it reflects real feedback rather than an assumption — which is the point of testing a first draft at all.

08 — Final Screens

Six screens.
One complete flow.

The final design is a 6-screen flow that handles the entire group ordering lifecycle — from initiation to individual payment confirmation.

01
Restaurant page
“Start group order” entry point sits alongside the regular “add to cart” button. It’s additive — the existing flow is untouched for solo users.
02
Invite screen
A shareable link plus a live list of who’s joined and what they’re currently doing (“Aisha is adding items”). Presence indicators reduce anxiety about whether people have seen the invite.
03
Live group cart
Each person adds their own items, visible to everyone in real time. Items are attributed by name. The cart shows a running total as items are added.
04
Lock order
A deliberate step before checkout. Prevents the cart from changing once people are ready. Creates a clear “this is final” moment that mirrors how real-world group decisions actually work.
05
Bill split (v2)
Default view shows the amount each person owes. Item detail is available on tap for anyone who wants to verify. Delivery fees and platform charges are split evenly and labelled clearly.
06
Payment confirmation
Each person pays their individual share rather than one person fronting the full amount. The order proceeds once all payments are confirmed.

10 — What I’d Test Next

This shipped
as a concept.

This was a personal project. There is no live usage data to report — I want to be direct about that rather than imply this shipped. What I have is directional signal from 5 interviews and 2 rounds of testing on the bill split screen specifically. If this were a real roadmap item:

01
Test the invite step specifically
The invite step is most likely to cause drop-off — getting a friend to click a link and add their items before the order locks. Does the link feel trustworthy enough to open, especially for someone without the app installed?
02
Decide what happens when someone is slow
The flow assumes everyone adds items promptly. In practice, someone will always be slow. Should there be a soft deadline? Should the order proceed without them? I’d want to test a couple of approaches rather than guess.
03
Validate the AOV hypothesis
The entire business case rests on smoother group ordering increasing average order value or frequency. Before investing further design time, I’d want to see if even a lightweight version of this feature (a shareable link with manual splitting) moves those metrics at all.
